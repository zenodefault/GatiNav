# Beyond Raw Sensor Data: A Hybrid Physics-AI Blueprint for Sub-10% Drift in Smartphone Dead Reckoning

## Analysis of Current State and Performance Bottlenecks

The development of an Intelligent Dead Reckoning (IDR) system that meets the stringent performance benchmark of less than 10% positional drift during GNSS outages represents a significant challenge in the field of consumer-grade navigation . The project's success hinges on the successful integration of six distinct technical capabilities into a cohesive system, deliverable as both a functional mobile application and an edge-deployable software engine . An honest assessment of the project's current state reveals a clear divergence between partial successes and critical failures, which directly explains the system's inability to meet its primary performance target. While foundational modules for alignment, filtering, and state management have been constructed, the core problem of along-track drift during vehicle motion remains fundamentally unsolved by the current methodology. This section provides a detailed analysis of the project's progress against its six required capabilities and identifies the systemic bottlenecks that are impeding progress toward the <10% drift goal.

The table below summarizes the status of each of the six required technical capabilities, drawing directly from the repository audit and preliminary analysis . This breakdown highlights where the team has succeeded in building functional components and, more importantly, where the architectural approach has led to critical failures. The overall trajectory shows that while the individual building blocks are being developed, their synergistic integration to solve the core dead reckoning problem is not yet effective.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| In-Vehicle Alignment & Calibration Engine | ⚠️ Partially Implemented / Failed Gate | The module functions under ideal conditions but fails in dynamic driving scenarios due to a lack of qualifying straight segments for yaw estimation and insufficient static calibration periods in the dataset . |

A deeper dive into these statuses reveals the project's central tension. The "GNSS+INS Fusion Engine" is described as "solid," indicating that the theoretical foundation for sensor fusion is well-understood and correctly implemented . It employs a 15-state error Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) that performs attitude leveling, processes GNSS position and velocity updates, and incorporates Zero Velocity Updates (ZUPT) and Non-Holonomic Constraints (NHC) as pseudo-measurements . This demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of tightly-coupled INS/GNSS integration, a technique used to mitigate drift by continuously correcting the inertial solution with global positioning data [[259](https://orbitalxploration.com/ins-gnss-integration-explained-how-imu-sensors-create-robust-navigation), [290](https://spectrum.ieee.org/inertial-navigation-system)]. However, the efficacy of this filter is entirely dependent on the quality of its inputs. The filter itself is not the source of the failure; rather, it is a victim of the poor quality of the upstream odometry data generated during motion .

Similarly, the "In-Vehicle Alignment & Calibration Engine" and "Advanced Map-Matching & Kinematic Constraints" show signs of progress but remain fragile. The alignment module, which is crucial for transforming sensor data from the phone's arbitrary coordinate frame to the vehicle's known driving frame, fails under realistic driving conditions . Its inability to find a qualifying straight segment to determine the yaw angle relative to the vehicle's heading means that all subsequent longitudinal acceleration measurements are compromised [[359](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.11120v2)]. Without accurate attitude compensation, the entire odometry calculation is built on a faulty premise. The map-matching component, while conceptually sound, struggles with practical implementation details. Initial failures were caused by a mismatch between the GNSS anchor's coordinate reference system (CRS) and the local map graph's CRS, rendering the matching algorithm impossible to execute . Even after this was fixed, the engagement rate remained low (4/9 windows), and the system was susceptible to locking onto wrong roads, a critical failure mode for a safety-related navigation system [[148](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364617212_Land_Vehicle_Navigation_Using_Low-Cost_Integrated_Smartphone_GNSS_Mems_and_Map_Matching_Technique)].

The most glaring failure is the "AI Speed & Vibration Filter." This capability is at the heart of the project's core challenge: estimating distance traveled without an external speedometer. The team's approach was to use a deep learning model to directly estimate forward velocity from noisy accelerometer and gyroscope inputs . This attempt failed catastrophically, producing a model whose predictions were uncorrelated with the ground truth and instead predicted the mean speed of the training data . This outcome is not merely a matter of poor model performance; it points to a fundamental limitation of the chosen approach. It suggests that the information content within the broadband vibration energy of the raw IMU signals is insufficient to reliably encode the vehicle's speed at a usable signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). This is a critical insight, as it implies that the problem statement itself contains a confounding factor—engine idling vibrations, pothole shocks, and other non-navigation motions—that makes a direct end-to-end mapping from raw sensor data to speed an ill-posed problem .

The consequence of these failures is starkly illustrated in the performance benchmark evaluation. During simulated GNSS outages, the system exhibits massive positional drift. For instance, in a 90-second outage window, the raw odometry produced a drift of 23,856 meters, and even after applying ZUPT and NHC, the drift was still 4,274 meters—a staggering 427% of the total distance traveled . Another 90-second window resulted in 5,778 meters of drift . These figures are orders of magnitude above the <10% drift requirement, which translates to a maximum of 500 meters over a 1-kilometer travel distance in a GNSS-denied environment . The user's own analysis confirms this dire situation, noting that the full pipeline (CNN + ZUPT + NHC) loses to plain ZUPT in aggregate drift across all windows (14,075 m vs 12,811 m) . This data unequivocally proves that the current system cannot function as intended. The problem is not a lack of components but a flawed strategy for combining them, particularly concerning the generation of odometry data during motion.

The project's phased, gate-driven protocol is a strength, as it forces a rigorous and transparent evaluation process . The failure at the "driving gate" in Phase 1 halted progress, and the subsequent attempts in Phases 4 and 5 did not address the root cause of the along-track drift issue . The "Seamless GNSS Deficit Handler," while logically sound in its design, remains untested in a real-time on-device context, representing a final piece of the puzzle that cannot be considered complete . The "Real-time Navigation Interface" is also incomplete, with only a basic record-and-replay application built so far . Therefore, the project is in a critical state: it possesses the necessary intellectual property for several components but lacks a working odometry solution that can generate reliable position estimates during motion, making the entire system non-functional for its primary purpose. The next phase of development must pivot away from the failed approaches and toward a fundamentally different strategy for generating odometry data.

## Diagnostic Assessment of the Core Odometry Failure

The catastrophic failure of the system's positional accuracy during GNSS outages stems from a single, deeply rooted problem: the inability to accurately compute along-track displacement while the vehicle is in motion. The user's own analysis correctly pinpoints the core issue: "Everything that works (ZUPT, EKF, matching, state machine) bounds lateral/heading/stopped drift — but along-track drift during motion is still open-loop double-integration, and every attempt to replace it with learned speed has hit the same identifiability wall" . This statement encapsulates the fundamental flaw in the initial approach and serves as the cornerstone for diagnosing the project's failure. To build a successful IDR system, it is imperative to understand why simple dead reckoning fails and why the proposed AI-based solutions, as implemented, are inadequate.

At its most basic level, dead reckoning calculates position by integrating acceleration data twice: once to get velocity and again to get position. This process is inherently unstable when performed with low-cost MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems) IMUs, which are standard in smartphones [[214](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2017/4517673)]. These sensors suffer from several intrinsic errors, including biases, scale factor inaccuracies, and high-frequency noise [[95](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10346868/), [214](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2017/4517673)]. A tiny, constant bias in the accelerometer reading, on the order of milli-gs, will integrate into a growing velocity error that, in turn, integrates into a position error that grows quadratically with time [[95](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10346868/)]. This is why a pure IMU solution results in rapid and uncontrollable drift, with typical systems drifting by thousands of meters in just 20 minutes of GNSS outage [[30](https://inertiallabs.com/tunnel-guide-feature-for-gps-aided-ins-improves-performance-during-gnss-outage/)]. The IO-VNBD dataset itself provides benchmarks showing that pure IMU dead reckoning yields extremely large 2D-RMSE values during short outage segments [[8](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/14/13/2423)]. The challenge is not theoretical; it is a physical reality of operating with imperfect sensors.

The first line of defense against this drift is the "AI Speed & Vibration Filter," an attempt to circumvent the double-integration problem by directly predicting velocity from IMU data. The team's approach was to train a deep learning model, specifically a CNN, to take raw IMU signals as input and output a prediction of forward velocity . This strategy, however, runs headlong into the "identifiability wall." As the user noted, the broadband energy from road noise, potholes, and engine vibrations does not reliably contain enough information to distinguish true vehicle speed from extraneous motion at a usable SNR . The resulting model's correlation with ground truth being nearly zero (`corr(pred, GT) = 0.054`) and its tendency to predict the mean speed of the dataset is a textbook symptom of this problem . The model has essentially learned that, given the overwhelming amount of non-linear noise, the most statistically probable prediction is simply the average speed observed in the training data. It has failed to learn the causal relationship between the sensor readings and the vehicle's actual motion because that relationship is obscured by confounding factors.

This failure highlights a critical misunderstanding of the problem space. The task is not merely to perform regression from sensor inputs to speed; it is to perform a very clean integration of an acceleration signal. A more robust approach would involve separating the problem into two parts: first, denoising the acceleration signal as much as physically possible, and second, performing the integration on this cleaner signal. Simply replacing one complex, poorly understood black-box model (the double integrator) with another (the speed predictor) does not solve the underlying physics problem. It merely shifts the point of failure. Furthermore, any model trained solely on IMU data is susceptible to the same issues of domain shift and environmental variation. A model trained in one city with a specific type of road surface may perform poorly in another city with different terrain and traffic patterns, leading to unpredictable drift.

An alternative, more conventional approach to mitigating drift during motion is the use of Zero Velocity Updates (ZUPT). The principle behind ZUPT is that if a vehicle is stationary (or moving at a very low, near-zero velocity), the integrated velocity should be zero. By detecting these stationary periods (e.g., at traffic lights or in parking lots) and feeding a "pseudo-measurement" of zero velocity into the EKF, the filter can correct accumulated errors [[170](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.16387)]. The team's implementation of ZUPT is functional, as evidenced by its ability to keep parked drift under control (achieving the target of <5m in a 5-minute park) . However, ZUPT is entirely ineffective during continuous motion. Its utility is limited to stopping-and-starting traffic scenarios and does nothing to constrain the drift that accumulates while the vehicle is traveling down a highway or through a tunnel. The system's failure during long, uninterrupted motion segments (e.g., W08 and W09 in the audit) is a direct result of the absence of valid ZUPT opportunities .

Therefore, the diagnosis of the core odometry failure is twofold:
1.  **Physics-Based Instability:** The fundamental process of double-integrating a biased, noisy MEMS accelerometer signal leads to exponential error growth. This is an unavoidable physical limitation of the sensor technology.
2.  **Methodological Flaw:** The attempted solution—a direct end-to-end neural network for speed prediction—failed because the input signal (raw IMU data) was too corrupted by noise and irrelevant motion to contain sufficient information for the task. The model could not overcome the "identifiability wall" created by engine harmonics, road vibrations, and sensor drift.

The implication is clear: any successful strategy must operate within the constraints of these realities. It cannot rely on a single, monolithic AI model to perform the entire odometry calculation from scratch. Instead, it must adopt a more nuanced, multi-stage approach that respects the physics of motion, uses ML to tackle specific, well-defined sub-problems (like noise modeling), and leverages external sources of information (like maps) to provide strong constraints on the vehicle's possible trajectory. The focus must shift from trying to invent a new kind of speed sensor to creating a more resilient and accurate method for integrating the available acceleration data. The IO-VNBD benchmark results suggest that such a method exists and involves a combination of attitude compensation, forward-axis projection, and targeted neural denoising [[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7907232/)].

## Strategic Pivot to a Hybrid Physics-Informed AI Approach

To overcome the profound challenges of drift and achieve the <10% positional accuracy benchmark, the project must abandon its current AI-centric approach to odometry and adopt a hybrid, physics-informed strategy. The evidence overwhelmingly indicates that attempting to train a deep learning model to directly predict speed from raw, noisy IMU data is a futile endeavor, as this data lacks the necessary signal-to-noise ratio to make such a prediction reliably . The path forward lies in leveraging the strengths of both classical inertial navigation theory and modern machine learning. This involves reframing the problem from "predicting speed" to "denoising acceleration," and structuring the solution as a multi-stage pipeline where physics-based calculations create clean features for a neural network to refine. This approach, inspired by the successful methodologies used in benchmarks like IO-VNBD, offers a more robust and scalable solution to the core dead reckoning problem [[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7907232/)].

The cornerstone of this strategic pivot is the adoption of a DRNet-style odometry architecture [[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7907232/)]. This paradigm separates the problem into two distinct stages: feature engineering based on physical principles, followed by targeted machine learning. This division of labor allows each component to do what it does best. First, the physical model handles the computationally intensive and deterministic tasks of attitude determination and axis alignment. Second, the neural network focuses on the stochastic and non-linear task of modeling the residual errors of the physical model.

The first stage, **Attitude Determination and Compensation**, is a well-established problem in inertial navigation [[360](https://www.steppeschool.com/blog/attitude-estimation-rotation-matrices-quaternions-sensor-fusion)]. The smartphone's accelerometer measures gravity and linear acceleration, while the magnetometer measures the Earth's magnetic field. By fusing these sensor readings using an Attitude and Heading Reference System (AHRS) filter, such as a Complementary Filter or an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF), it is possible to compute the device's orientation (roll, pitch, and yaw) relative to the world's coordinate frame [[361](https://www.mathworks.com/help/fusion/gs/determine-orientation-through-sensor-fusion.html), [362](https://openimu.readthedocs.io/en/latest/apps/ahrs.html)]. This solved problem is critical because it transforms all subsequent sensor measurements from the phone's arbitrary coordinate system into the vehicle's consistent body-fixed frame [[359](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.11120v2)]. Once the phone's pose is known, the accelerometer's measurement can be projected onto the vehicle's forward axis. This projection isolates the component of acceleration related purely to longitudinal motion (acceleration/deceleration), while ideally rejecting the components related to vertical bumps (pitch) and lateral cornering (roll). This step is paramount, as it ensures that the signal fed into the odometry engine is relevant to the task of distance estimation. Without this compensation, errors in the phone's mounting angle lead to significant cross-axis coupling, contaminating the forward acceleration signal and causing erroneous velocity and position estimates [[167](https://electronics.stackexchange.com/questions/231779/measuring-vehicles-forward-and-lateral-acceleration-using-a-smartphone)].

The second stage, **Neural Residual Learning**, is where machine learning provides a powerful enhancement. Instead of asking a neural network to learn the entire complex relationship from raw IMU data to speed, the task is simplified dramatically. The input to the neural network becomes the attitude-compensated forward acceleration signal. The network's sole responsibility is to learn the *residual error* of this signal—essentially, the difference between the measured (but compensated) acceleration and the true acceleration of the vehicle. This is a much easier and more stable learning problem because the desired signal is already present in the input; the network need only model the complex, non-linear noise profile of the specific MEMS IMU being used [[263](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Novel-Approach-for-IMU-Denoising-using-Machine-Damagatla-Atia/1f8ad1a33a964279e831dfb5a4588fbcb721d3ae), [266](https://github.com/ansfl/MEMS-IMU-Denoising)]. A lightweight neural network architecture, such as a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) or a simple Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with appropriate temporal context, is well-suited for this task [[50](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/1/181), [138](https://iopscience.iop.org/issue/2631-8695/7/4)]. The model would be trained using supervised learning on the IO-VNBD dataset, where ground truth acceleration can be derived from high-precision wheel encoder data or RTK-GNSS data also recorded in the dataset [[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7907232/), [242](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/3/833)]. The loss function would be the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the network's predicted residual and the actual ground truth residual. The output of this network is a refined acceleration value, which is then fed into the integration block.

The final stage is **Integration and Fusion**. The denoised acceleration signal is integrated once to produce a more accurate velocity estimate and a second time to produce a more accurate position estimate. This cleaned trajectory is then propagated through the main EKF. The EKF's propagation step uses this improved odometry model instead of the raw accelerometer data. The measurement update steps of the EKF, which fuse this odometry with periodic GNSS fixes and ZUPT events, can now operate on a much more reliable state estimate, leading to significantly better overall performance. This hybrid approach combines the interpretability and physical correctness of the attitude compensation with the adaptive, data-driven power of the neural denoiser, creating a system that is more robust to sensor imperfections and environmental variations than either approach could be alone.

This strategic pivot addresses the core weaknesses of the previous methodology. It acknowledges the physical limits of the sensor and works *with* them rather than trying to ignore them. It moves beyond the ill-posed problem of direct speed prediction to a well-posed problem of signal denoising. And it provides a clear, modular architecture where each component can be developed, tested, and optimized independently. By implementing this DRNet-style odometry pipeline, the project can finally begin to meaningfully attack the problem of along-track drift during motion, laying the groundwork for a system that stands a genuine chance of meeting the demanding performance targets.

## Actionable Implementation Roadmap for Odometry and Fusion

To transition from the failed current state to a functioning hybrid physics-AI system, a concrete and actionable implementation roadmap is required. This roadmap prioritizes the most critical change—the replacement of the failed speed-prediction model—with a DRNet-style odometry pipeline. Each step must be executed with precision, leveraging the rich data available in the IO-VNBD dataset to ensure the new components are properly trained and validated. The following plan outlines the essential modifications to the existing codebase and the creation of new modules necessary to achieve the project's goals.

**Phase 1: Reframe and Rebuild the Odometry Pipeline (Core Task)**

The single most important action is to discard the existing `4th-output CNN speed head` and its associated training loop, as it has proven to be non-viable . This frees up resources to implement the superior DRNet-style approach.

1.  **Implement Robust Attitude Compensation Module:**
    *   **Action:** Enhance the existing `In-Vehicle Alignment & Calibration Engine`. This module must reliably compute the rotation matrix that transforms vectors from the phone's body frame to the vehicle's body frame. This involves solving for the three Euler angles: pitch, roll, and yaw.
    *   **Methodology:** Use an AHRS filter that fuses the accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer data. The accelerometer provides a gravity vector to determine pitch and roll, while the magnetometer provides a magnetic north vector to resolve the yaw ambiguity and provide absolute heading [[360](https://www.steppeschool.com/blog/attitude-estimation-rotation-matrices-quaternions-sensor-fusion), [361](https://www.mathworks.com/help/fusion/gs/determine-orientation-through-sensor-fusion.html)]. The filter should be initialized using the static period of data before an outage occurs, aligning the phone's z-axis with the gravity vector and its y-axis with magnetic north. During motion, the gyroscope propagates the orientation forward in time, while the accelerometer and magnetometer provide corrective feedback to prevent drift [[362](https://openimu.readthedocs.io/en/latest/apps/ahrs.html)].
    *   **Output:** The module should output a quaternion or a 3x3 rotation matrix that is applied to all incoming accelerometer and gyroscope data in real-time.

2.  **Develop the Neural Residual Learning Network:**
    *   **Action:** Create a new, lightweight neural network model designed to learn the residual error of the forward-axis accelerometer projection.
    *   **Model Architecture:** A Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) is a strong candidate due to its efficiency and ability to capture temporal dependencies [[138](https://iopscience.iop.org/issue/2631-8695/7/4)]. Alternatively, a shallow MLP with a sliding window of recent accelerometer data points can suffice [[139](https://iopscience.iop.org/issue/0957-0233/36/1)]. The input dimension will be the number of past/future timesteps, and the output dimension will be 1 (a scalar correction for the forward acceleration).
    *   **Data Preparation:** Using the IO-VNBD dataset, extract the raw accelerometer data. Apply the attitude compensation rotation matrix computed in Step 1 to project the 3D acceleration vector onto the vehicle's forward axis. This is the network's input. The ground truth label for this input signal can be obtained by differentiating the high-precision wheel encoder data or the GNSS-derived velocity from the same dataset. The residual is calculated as: $Residual = (Input\_Acceleration + GroundTruth\_Acceleration) - Input\_Acceleration$. The network learns to predict this residual.
    *   **Training:** Train the network using a mean squared error (MSE) loss function. The optimizer should be something efficient like Adam. Training should be done offline on the cloud/desktop, as per the project protocol .

3.  **Integrate Denoising into the Propagation Loop:**
    *   **Action:** Modify the core of the GNSS+INS Fusion Engine. The EKF's propagation step, which currently integrates raw accelerometer data, must be updated.
    *   **Workflow:** At each IMU sample timestamp, perform the following sequence:
        a. Receive raw accelerometer reading from the phone.
        b. Apply the real-time attitude compensation rotation matrix to get the vehicle-frame acceleration.
        c. Project this vector onto the forward axis to get a scalar `forward_accel_raw`.
        d. Pass `forward_accel_raw` through the trained neural network to get a residual correction `residual_pred`.
        e. Calculate the denoised acceleration: $forward\_accel\_denoised = forward\_accel\_raw - residual\_pred$.
        f. Integrate `forward_accel_denoised` (twice) to propagate the position and velocity states in the EKF.
    *   **Validation:** This new odometry chain must be validated in isolation before being reintegrated into the full EKF. Simulate a GNSS outage and compare the drift of the new denoised odometry against the raw odometry and the old CNN-based speed estimator.

**Phase 2: Enhance Fusion Logic and Transition Handling**

With a more robust odometry source, the rest of the fusion system can be tuned for better performance.

1.  **Augment the EKF with Stronger Pseudo-Measurements:**
    *   **Action:** Strengthen the use of ZUPT and NHC within the EKF.
    *   **Methodology:** Improve the ZUPT detector to be more robust, potentially using a combination of low-speed thresholds and accelerometer variance to detect true stops versus slow, rolling movement. For NHC, implement a mechanism where the EKF doesn't just apply a small correction but actively constrains the state covariance in directions perpendicular to the road. When a confident map-matching result is available, the innovation (the difference between the INS position and the snapped position) can be used to update the filter gain, allowing for larger corrections when the constraint is strong [[225](http://www.navlab.iit.edu/uploads/5/9/7/3/59735535/kana_2022_gnss.pdf)].

2.  **Refine the Seamless GNSS Deficit Handler:**
    *   **Action:** Finalize and test the state machine for real-time on-device execution.
    *   **Logic:** The state machine should monitor GNSS signal health metrics (e.g., PDOP, number of satellites, signal-to-noise ratio). When these metrics fall below a certain threshold, the system transitions from the `FUSED` state to a `REACQUIRING` state. In this state, it might temporarily increase the process noise in the EKF to be more responsive to new measurements. If the GNSS signal returns and passes integrity checks, it smoothly transitions back to `FUSED`. The transition between `FUSED` and an `INERTIAL` state (pure dead reckoning) should be instantaneous, relying on the robustness of the new odometry model to bridge the gap [[66](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12471993/)].

By systematically executing this roadmap, the project can move beyond its current impasse. The focus shifts from chasing a flawed AI solution to building a principled, physics-aware system that leverages AI as a tool for refinement, not as a magic bullet for a broken problem. This approach directly targets the root cause of the drift and provides a clear path toward meeting the project's ambitious performance requirements.

## Leveraging Map-Matching as a Primary Kinematic Constraint

While improving the odometry model is the most critical step, achieving the <10% drift benchmark in representative driving scenarios will require treating map-matching not as a secondary refinement tool, but as a primary, active constraint that guides and corrects the inertial trajectory throughout the entire GNSS outage. The current passive snapping approach, which only acts when the estimated position is already close to a road, is insufficient to suppress the significant along-track drift that accumulates from imperfect odometry . A more robust strategy involves using the road network geometry to enforce kinematic constraints, particularly Non-Holonomic Constraints (NHC), continuously during an outage. This approach is especially potent in structured environments like tunnels and highways, where the road layout provides strong, unambiguous guidance [[148](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364617212_Land_Vehicle_Navigation_Using_Low-Cost_Integrated_Smartphone_GNSS_Mems_and_Map_Matching_Technique), [202](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/14/10/4309)].

The fundamental principle of map-matching is to probabilistically associate a noisy sequence of GNSS or INS-derived positions with the nearest path on a digital road network [[90](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352146524003430)]. However, in a GNSS-denied scenario, the "input" to the map-matching algorithm is the highly uncertain inertial trajectory. The goal shifts from correcting a slightly-off GNSS fix to correcting a rapidly diverging INS solution. This requires a more aggressive and intelligent matching strategy. The IO-VNBD benchmark methodology provides a valuable precedent, suggesting that advanced techniques like those based on Hidden Markov Models (HMM) are essential for real-time performance on resource-constrained devices [[85](https://kth.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:2067114/FULLTEXT01.pdf), [87](https://kth.diva-portal.org/smash/record.jsf?pid=diva2:2067114)].

A key improvement is to transform map-matching into a continuous state estimation problem. Instead of a discrete "snap-to-road" operation, the map-matching algorithm should generate a continuous stream of "pseudo-measurements" that act as constraints on the EKF's state. When the system determines it is likely on a specific road segment, it can calculate the lateral deviation of the INS-predicted position from that segment's centerline. This lateral deviation, along with its uncertainty, can be treated as a measurement and fed into the EKF's update step. This process, often called "soft constraint" or "constrained filtering," pulls the state estimate back toward the physically plausible path defined by the map, preventing it from drifting off the road entirely [[65](https://d-nb.info/1390874400/34), [225](http://www.navlab.iit.edu/uploads/5/9/7/3/59735535/kana_2022_gnss.pdf)]. This is a form of NHC, which assumes a car cannot slide sideways or fly upwards, and leverages the road geometry to enforce this constraint [[42](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/19/24/5430)].

To implement this effectively, several enhancements to the current framework are necessary:

1.  **Dynamic and Localized Map Graph Loading:** The initial failure of the map-matching component was partly due to a coordinate system mismatch between the GNSS anchor and the loaded map graph . This highlights the need for a robust map management system. The solution is to implement a dynamic graph loading mechanism. At the precise moment a GNSS outage is detected, the system should query an offline map database (e.g., OpenStreetMap) to download a map tile centered on the last known, valid GNSS position [[85](https://kth.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:2067114/FULLTEXT01.pdf)]. This ensures perfect alignment between the inertial trajectory's starting point and the local road network graph. For efficiency, the graph should be pre-processed into a format suitable for fast pathfinding algorithms.

2.  **Probabilistic Matching with an HMM:** The team has already adopted an HMM-based framework, which is a good foundation . To make it robust, the HMM's emission probabilities must accurately model the likelihood of observing a particular inertial state (position, velocity) given that the vehicle is on a specific road segment. The transition probabilities should favor continuation along a segment and logical turns, penalizing illegal maneuvers or u-turns. The Viterbi algorithm can then be used to find the most likely sequence of road segments that corresponds to the entire inertial trajectory since the last GNSS fix [[85](https://kth.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:2067114/FULLTEXT01.pdf)]. This gives a much more globally consistent result than simple greedy snapping.

3.  **Aggressive NHC Enforcement in Structured Scenarios:** Since the project goal allows for prioritization of representative driving scenarios, the system can be made smarter about when to apply strong constraints . The logic can be enhanced to detect when the vehicle is likely in a tunnel or on a highway, perhaps based on GNSS signal history, speed profiles, or even by fusing data from other sensors if available. When such a scenario is detected, the map-matching module can engage stronger NHC pseudo-measurements. For example, the filter's process noise could be increased, and the measurement noise of the NHC update could be decreased, forcing the state estimate to adhere more tightly to the linear, constrained path of the road. This targeted approach maximizes the benefit of map-matching where it is most effective.

4.  **Robust Snap-Residual Gating:** The current implementation uses a snap-residual gate to decide whether to trust a match . This is a crucial safety mechanism. The gate should be made stricter to prevent the system from locking onto a completely incorrect road. The system should be designed to fail gracefully, perhaps reverting to a less constrained dead-reckoning-only mode or triggering a warning if the map-matching confidence drops below a safe threshold. This trade-off—losing a few matches to avoid catastrophic lock-in—is a necessary part of building a robust system [[335](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.03868v2)].

By elevating map-matching from a post-processing step to an integral part of the real-time state estimation process, the system can leverage the rich contextual information of the road network to counteract the inherent drift of the IMU. This hybrid approach, combining a physics-informed, AI-enhanced odometry model with strong, continuous kinematic constraints from the map, represents the most promising path toward achieving the project's demanding performance goals.

## Deployment Strategy for On-Device and Edge Environments

The final deliverable requires the system to function as both a mobile application for smartphones and a flexible edge-deployable engine capable of processing data from various IMUs, including external FOG-based units . This dual-deployment requirement necessitates a carefully architected software stack that is modular, efficient, and adaptable. The strategy for deployment must focus on model optimization for resource-constrained on-device execution while ensuring the core logic remains agnostic to the specific IMU hardware.

For the **mobile application**, the primary challenges are computational latency, memory footprint, and power consumption. Running a real-time EKF with a neural network inference step on a smartphone demands extreme efficiency. The recommended update rate for the mobile app is 10Hz . To meet this requirement, the newly developed neural network for residual acceleration learning must undergo a process called quantization. Quantization is the process of converting a normally floating-point (e.g., 32-bit float) model into a lower-precision integer format (e.g., 8-bit integer, INT8) [[332](https://docs.plumerai.com/2.0/inference_engine/example_models/LSTM/)]. This transformation dramatically reduces the model's size and allows it to be executed on specialized hardware accelerators within modern smartphone SoCs, such as Neural Processing Units (NPUs) [[390](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/edge-ai-chips-turning-smartphones-powerhouses-mario-camaj-weeqe)]. Qualcomm's AI Hub Workbench, for example, provides tools to convert and compile models for on-device execution [[333](https://workbench.aihub.qualcomm.com/docs/hub/quantize_examples.html)]. Studies on TinyML show that even with potential minor accuracy losses, quantized models can achieve inference latencies as low as 2ms, making them perfectly suited for real-time applications [[92](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12196908/)]. The training itself can occur on a desktop/cloud environment using standard floating-point arithmetic, with quantization applied as a final compilation step for deployment [[332](https://docs.plumerai.com/2.0/inference_engine/example_models/LSTM/)]. This ensures the AI models are lightweight and can run efficiently alongside the rest of the application.

The **edge-deployable software engine** must be designed for flexibility and higher performance. The specification calls for supporting IMU data at a higher rate (around 200Hz) when using external sensors . The core architecture of the engine should mirror that of the mobile application but with greater computational headroom. The modularity of the system, with distinct components for attitude compensation, neural denoising, and EKF fusion, is key here. The engine should be built around a standardized data structure for IMU measurements, containing fields for timestamp, accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer readings. This abstraction layer decouples the core navigation logic from the data acquisition hardware. Consequently, the exact same C++ or Python implementation of the DRNet-style odometry pipeline and the EKF can be instantiated and fed data from either the smartphone's internal MEMS IMU or a high-performance external IMU connected via USB or serial port [[344](https://github.com/open-edge-platform/edge-ai-libraries), [345](https://github.com/awesomelistsio/awesome-ai-edge-computing)]. The only parameter that would need adjustment is the integration timestep, which would be shorter for the 200Hz data stream compared to the typical smartphone sampling rate. This approach fulfills the project requirement of having a unified set of algorithms that work across both deployment targets. The edge engine can also leverage more powerful computing platforms, potentially GPUs, to run more complex models or perform additional computations, such as real-time 3D visual localization or LiDAR fusion, if those capabilities are added in the future [[327](https://introl.com/blog/autonomous-vehicle-ai-infrastructure-edge-cloud-gpu-requirements), [329](https://www.maris-tech.com/blog/edge-computing-in-autonomous-vehicles-for-real-time-defense-ai/)].

Finally, the **real-time navigation interface** must be polished and made robust. The initial Android application (Phase A) successfully records and replays data, which is invaluable for development and debugging . Phase B of the mobile application development must focus on creating a seamless, real-time user experience. This involves integrating the finalized EKF and TFLite model into a live Kotlin application that subscribes to the phone's sensor data stream. The UI should display a smooth, animated vehicle icon that reflects the fused GNSS/INS position, providing immediate feedback to the user. Adherence to in-vehicle UI standards, such as ISO 15008, is crucial for ensuring the display is legible and minimizes driver distraction [[269](https://www.iso.org/obp/ui/#iso:std:iso:15008:en), [270](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/cen/8cdb0d17-7535-4356-b846-a6a526ea1c8a/en-iso-15008-2017?srsltid=AfmBOoos3AZssjWKTZrcFdBrN2tRZPWlv5RA6BIu3VfsjtCn51H99mIA)]. Best practices for integrating with navigation SDKs, such as those provided by Google Maps, can offer valuable guidance on designing intuitive turn-by-turn instructions and map displays [[258](https://mapsplatform.google.com/resources/blog/best-practices-for-integrating-with-the-navigation-sdk-for-android/), [281](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/navigation/android-sdk/release-notes)]. The entire workflow, from sensor input to on-screen display, must be optimized to run smoothly at the target 10Hz update rate, ensuring the vehicle's location is always current and the navigation feels responsive and trustworthy [[287](https://mapsplatform.google.com/resources/blog/enhance-driver-awareness-and-engagement-with-real-time-disruptions-in-navigation-sdk/)]. By focusing on these deployment-specific considerations, the project can successfully transition from a collection of functional modules to a polished, robust, and deployable system that meets all specified requirements.
